Which pages should an editor review first?

A grouped-validation refresh-opportunity playbook for Search Console data — built on the FlyRank ML Internship dataset.

On this page Abstract 1 · Introduction 2 · Data 3 · Methodology 4 · Results 5 · Limitations 6 · Recommendations 7 · Reproducibility 8 · Acknowledgments

Abstract

A content editor can only review a handful of a portfolio's already-visible pages in a week, so something has to decide which pages get looked at first. Using March 2026 Search Console facts and an April 2026 decline outcome from the FlyRank ML Internship warehouse release — 100,893 anonymized pages across 43 clients — I trained a Random Forest and scored it against a transparent rule baseline on the same held-out clients under a grouped-by-client design. On 13 held-out clients the forest ranked about-to-decline pages at precision@50 0.86, precision@100 0.87 and ROC-AUC 0.739, against 0.44 and 0.451 for the rule at a 0.429 base rate. The strongest driver was recent impression momentum, a deliberate leak probe inflated precision@50 to 1.00 once a future-month feature was added, and a five-fold grouped check held at precision@50 0.944 ± 0.023. The result is shipped as a short, human-reviewed refresh queue with reason codes, explicit limits and monitoring triggers — decision support, not a prediction about Google's algorithm.

1 · Introduction / Problem Statement

An editor at a content agency does not have a discovery problem; the portfolio already has thousands of pages with real impressions. The problem is allocation: the review budget is a few hundred pages per week while the library holds tens of thousands. Ranking every page by raw impressions or by "declining" alone spends that budget on pages that are either already healthy or too small to matter.

The decision this analysis supports is concrete and recurring: given a fixed weekly review budget, which already-visible pages should an editor open first — and why? Getting it wrong in one direction wastes scarce human hours on pages nobody needed; getting it wrong in the other lets a page one quietly decay while a competitor takes the click.

This is a ranking / scoring problem, not classification. The output is not a yes/no label but a priority order that an editor works down from the top, so the metric that matters is precision at the top of the queue — not overall accuracy, which is dominated by the tens of thousands of rows the editor will never open.

2 · Data

Two scales of the same public, anonymized internship data are used, and every result in this paper is labelled with the scale it comes from.

Full release (the headline numbers)

  • Source: FlyRank/internship-warehouse on Hugging Face (public-but-gated; a read token is needed to fetch it). Table fact_content_daily_performance, partitioned by month.
  • Unit of analysis: one row per page, aggregated over a month.
  • Decision window: March 2026 features. Outcome window: April 2026 — a page is labelled declined_next_30d when its April impressions fall below 80% of March.
  • Scope: pages with at least 100 March impressions and Search Console reporting — the "already visible" pool. Final pool: 100,893 pages across 43 clients; whole-pool base rate 0.515.

Bundled slice (offline reproduction)

data/raw/content_refresh_anonymized.csv — 30,000 anonymized pages, 44 columns, shipped with the starter repo. It carries only a same-window proxy label (trend_direction == "down"), so it is used to reproduce the method offline, never to claim the headline numbers.

Privacy. Only hashed page and client ids appear, and the shipped artifacts contain aggregates and buckets. No client names, domains, URLs, search queries, credentials or raw exports are used or published anywhere in this project.

Exclusions (and why)

  • Pages below the impression threshold — out of scope by design; this playbook reviews pages that are already being seen.
  • GA4 engagement as a population filter — it is sparse, and filtering on it would silently drop pages; it is kept only as a feature with an explicit "unknown" value.
  • Content-release dates as features — the release snapshot is as-of-release and would leak the future into the model. This is audited in §3.

3 · Methodology

Features

Eighteen numeric signals plus eight categorical page descriptors, all taken from the trailing March window: impression/click/session aggregates (log-scaled), CTR, average position, days-with-impressions coverage, content age and days-since-update, depth (word/char counts), and engagement rates. Four features ultimately carry the model (Figure 3); the rest add robustness.

Label and baseline

The label is the forward outcome declined_next_30d described above. The baseline is a transparent, hand-written rule — flag page-one pages whose CTR sits in the bottom half of their position band, weighted by impression exposure — so the comparison is against a rule an editor could apply by hand, not a straw man.

Validation design

Portfolios are the natural unit of generalization: a refresh playbook is expected to work on a new client. Both the baseline and every model are therefore scored on the same held-out whole clients (a seed-42 grouped split; 30 clients train, 13 held out, 20,002 held-out pages, base rate 0.429). A naive random 70/30 split is run alongside it purely to show how much client leakage inflates the score, and a five-fold GroupKFold is run to check stability across different held-out client sets.

Models and leakage checks

Logistic Regression is the readable anchor; a Random Forest (400 trees, min_samples_leaf=15) is the strong learner. Every feature is re-checked for when it is knowable: anything derived from the labelling window is banned. Two probes make the risk concrete — a leak probe that adds one future-month feature, and an offline probe on the bundled slice that adds one label-derived column — both of which are shown in §4 rather than hidden.

Stability and leakage checks

A five-fold GroupKFold over whole clients puts the forest at P@50 = 0.944 ± 0.023 (AUC 0.751 ± 0.024), so the headline is not an artefact of one lucky seed. Two probes then bound the result from the other side:

  • The honest-vs-naive gap. A random 70/30 split jumps to P@50 0.92 with all 39 clients appearing on both sides; the grouped split scores 0.86 with zero client overlap. Client leakage is worth roughly six points of false confidence.
  • The leak trap. Adding a single future-month feature drives P@50 to 1.00 and AUC to 0.995. That is the score you get from cheating; it is reported here so the headline 0.86 can be read as honest.
Reproduction at the offline scale. The bundled 30,000-page slice uses a same-window proxy label instead of a genuine outcome. There, a good CTR-for-position rule and the learned models roughly tie, and the random-split inflation is even larger (P@50 0.98 with all 31 test clients leaking into train versus 0.64 grouped). Wilson-smaller data and a weaker label are exactly the conditions under which a transparent rule holds up — which is why the forward-label release, not the proxy slice, carries the headline.

5 · Limitations and Honest Framing

  • Decision support, not prediction or cause. The queue orders pages for human review. It does not forecast a page's traffic and it does not establish that refreshing a flagged page causes recovery — that requires a controlled experiment, not an observational ranking.
  • Association, not mechanism. "Momentum dominates" is a measured relationship between trailing impressions and the forward decline label. It is directional evidence, not a claim about Google's ranking algorithm, and it does not prove any ranking signal exists.
  • One month, one market. A single decision window (March 2026) and 43 clients. GroupKFold stability helps, but cross-month replication is the honest next step before treating the effect sizes as constants.
  • Proxy at the bundled scale. The offline slice's label is a same-window definition; its numbers reproduce the method and are labelled as a proxy everywhere they appear.
  • Scores are rankings, not calibrated probabilities. The queue is re-issued each month and must not be reused once the next month closes.

6 · Ranked Recommendations — the action playbook

The model score is converted into four actions by percentile, so the queue is deliberately far smaller than the pool. The review-first tier is 1,009 pages — 1.0% of the pool, covering 0.8% of March demand, and 80.7% of those pages are already on page one: visible, but not earning the click.

Every review-first row carries feature-only reason codes, so an editor can check the "why" before spending time. In the review-first tier the dominant signal is a page that is both under-earning clicks and already decelerating.

How to use it. Work the review-first tier top-down, check the reason code, and act by hand; keep the watch tier for drafting. Re-issue the queue monthly and watch two triggers — if the base rate drifts outside its expected band, or held-out precision@50 falls below 0.75, retrain before trusting the next queue.
